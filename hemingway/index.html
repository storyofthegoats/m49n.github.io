The Hemingway Home

  • The house

    Built in 1851 from stone quarried on the grounds. Pauline Pfeiffer’s uncle bought the house for her and Ernest in 1931, and the couple restored it.

  • The writing studio

    Hemingway wrote in the studio on the property. For a sense of the town that fed his work, start with To Have and Have Not, his novel about a boat captain making a living in Depression-era Key West.

  • Pauline’s pool

    The pool went in during 1937–38 where Hemingway’s boxing ring had stood. A penny is set into the cement at the north end, where the museum tells a story about an expensive project and Hemingway’s last cent.

  • The six-toed cats

    The museum traces its cat lore to Snow White, a six-toed cat said to have been a gift from a sea captain. Many of the cats on the grounds today have extra toes.

  • Out on the Pilar

    Hemingway kept his fishing boat a few blocks from the house. He and friends including Joe Russell and Charles Thompson fished the waters around the Dry Tortugas, Bimini, and Cuba.

A Goose That Could Tell Time

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

At five every afternoon the goose crossed the road in front of the bus. The driver said it was because the goose liked to stop traffic. A man who worked at the mill said it crossed at four fifty-seven before the timetable changed. We tested this by asking the driver to turn up early.

He turned up early with seven people in the bus. They waited while the goose stood at the fence. At five a woman in a blue coat came through the mill gate with a bucket and the goose crossed. The driver opened the door. She stepped on and put the bucket between her feet.

“Is it always waiting for you?” he asked.

“Only since it missed me once,” she said.

The Things We Kept Dry

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 6 min read

On the third morning the water came up through the floor of the shop. A man in a blue apron took his shoes off before going in after a box of paper. He came out holding it above his head. The cardboard was white still at the top and black at the bottom. He set it on the bonnet of a car and stood there in his socks. He asked if I would put one hand underneath while he took the lid off.

The water smelled of wet bricks and something sweet that had come out of the bakery. The street had been open the day before and a bus had come through with the water up over one hub. In the morning no buses came. There was a woman waiting for one at the top of the street. She kept looking uphill though the buses always came from the other way.

The man had shut the shop on the first day and put a note in the window saying when he would be back. He went over and took it down. He had to reach through the flap where people put the post. He got it out in two pieces and tucked them under the wiper of the car. He said he had bought the car to go places on Sundays. It had not been out of the street since the brakes went.

The shop sold secondhand things that had lost their other halves. He had the saucers for two lines of cups and a bin of brass handles with no screws. Under the clock he had three shelves of photographs. People asked who the children in the pictures were. Sometimes he made up a name and sometimes it came to him that there had been a time when everybody in a picture had known the name and now nobody in the room did.

There was a man who had come in every month to look at the photographs. He had lost one that he believed had been taken at the station. There were six men in it and somebody had written the name of the station across the bottom. He used to think he would know the picture by the sixth man because that man had stood with one foot on the platform and one on the step of the train. The shopkeeper had asked which one was him. He said he was the one who took it.

One spring he brought in a photograph of a wall with a clock on it and said he was certain it had been the same day. The clock showed a quarter to eight. After he left, the shopkeeper held it to the light and saw where someone had scratched out the photograph company's name. He kept it in the till until the man came again. When he came the next month he said he'd found the negative of the train and no longer had anything that could print it. The shopkeeper said to bring it in next time.

The box held papers from the desk. On top there was the deed to the shop and a bill for the coal grate. Under that was a packet of photographs bound by yellow string. I asked if any of them were his. He said only the one at the bottom and he had put it there to keep the others flat. We got the packet out on a dry part of the car. The top pictures were dry. Two at the bottom had caught the damp and stuck together.

A woman from the paint store brought a sheet of masonite. She set one end on the bonnet and one on a wheelbarrow and we laid out the pictures. There was a dog wearing a handkerchief. There was a man by a bicycle that leaned against a wall. The tires had the heavy white rubber that had been good then. In one there was nothing but a small patch of yard and a line hung with white shirts. You could see that the shirts were wet by the weight of the sleeves. I asked him whether he wanted to put the damp ones in the sun. He said to wait until we got them apart.

The woman asked if there was any more paper in the shop. He said there was a drawer of letters under the desk and that it had swollen shut. She wanted to go in with a screwdriver. He said the floor had gone soft between the counter and the desk. She took a long board from her store and put it against the wall where the water was shallower. He went in on it and got his fingers under the edge of the drawer. The woman kept her boot on the dry end of the board. We could hear small things dropping inside the drawer.

He came out with nothing. When he got back to the car he said the letters were from a man who used to own the building and had kept writing him after he sold it. For the first year every letter had asked whether the clock still kept time. Eventually they had quit asking about the clock. I asked him if he had answered them. He said he had sent a Christmas card twice and on the second one he had written that it lost four minutes a week.

He went over to the paint store and came back with some packing tape. He taped the note to the glass, blank side out, and wrote that he had set up on the corner. There was no address on it. There was only one corner on that block above water.

The woman brought a tray from the shop and put clean water in it. We let the pictures float. The nearer one began to lift at a corner. We stood a long time over the tray. A truck came along and its tires threw water almost to the tops of the doors. The man yelled once at the driver and then someone in the truck yelled that the road behind him was washed out. The man from the shop told him to stay there and wait for a cable. Then he came back to the tray. The corners of the pictures had gone apart on their own.

The driver was young and asked if he could come out on our side. The man told him to wind the window down and see how far he could reach with his foot. He could reach the running board and the water was coming up over it. The woman went to the corner and shouted for the man with the tow van. There was no answer. She shouted again and a dog began barking from an upper window. Then the van came down to the bus stop and the woman who had been waiting there pointed it toward us.

One was of the shop when the building had a cloth awning and sold boots. His picture was of a boy with a long piece of wood on his shoulder. Beyond the boy a woman had come out under the awning. The man said he'd been sent to bring it in before the rain. On the back, in a hand that had got into the paper and come through it, somebody had written that he'd brought it in the wrong way. He was happy to find the words still there. He asked the woman from the paint store to hold the masonite level while he went to see about the man in the truck. There was a good dry spot on the bonnet beside my hand. I moved his picture there with the others.

I bought an old picture of a ball club and the dealer folded it to get it in the envelope. It was my own fault for buying something wider than my postbox. On the crease seven men went through at the shoulder and a boy with a bat went through at the waist.

I wrote the dealer a letter and tore it up. Instead I ordered seven tiny frames. The boy went into one with the bottom folded back the opposite way and showed up whole. The Borrowed Chorus

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

The band played the chorus three times because a woman by the door was still singing it. She had come in to get out of the rain and didn't know the verses. I sang the first line of the next verse to show her where it went. She said she knew where it went.

She waited until the chorus came round and sang again. The man on the bass watched her and took his hands off the strings just before the last line. You could hear her clear across the room.

Outside she opened an umbrella with one rib gone. She stood under it a while, waiting for the rest of the rain.

The Wrong Kind of Quiet

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

The library bought a machine to measure noise. It sat under the clock and lit up red every time the second hand stepped over the top. I pointed this out to the woman at the desk. She took the clock down.

At noon the machine lit up when an old man opened a book. The book made a crack halfway through. He turned the page carefully and it did it again. The woman came over and laid the book flat so he could see where the binding was loose.

They read quietly for some time. The red light went on when the coffee she'd left in the microwave clicked off. She covered the machine with a folded newspaper.

The Road of the Empty Trailer

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

I followed a trailer most of the way across the plain. It was empty and you could tell by the way it jumped. I could have passed it until the road narrowed and by then I had begun watching the chain under it to see if it would break.

At the filling station the driver got out and hung the loose chain on the hitch. He said he'd heard it dragging. He had been to pick up a boat and the man who owned it had changed his mind. The driver said it was a good boat and the man was right.

I went on ahead and drove half an hour without seeing anything I wanted to stop for. Then I stopped for coffee and waited until he went by.

Closing Weight

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

The bakery was changing hands. They had agreed to sell all the bread at the usual price on the last day, but when a loaf came in light by two ounces the old owner wrapped it in its own paper and set it aside. He meant to eat it for supper.

The new owner tried the scale and got the same result. Then she put two ounces of butter on the paper. He took it off and put it on the counter. She put it back.

They sold the last good loaf after dark. A little later I walked by and saw them at the bench with the light loaf cut open between them. There was butter on both knives.

The Piano with One Pedal

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 2 min read

The piano had only the loud pedal. The man who played it said the soft pedal was in the drawer with the forks. He had put it there after a supper when somebody told him he didn't have to play so loudly.

I'd come to the supper with a man who said he had once sold that piano. He remembered carrying it in through the front and going back for the bench, which had gone to another house on the same truck. The woman who bought it sat on a chair from the dining room for twenty years. When the bench finally arrived she put plants on it. After the supper I looked along the lid and could see the brown rings from the pots.

While we cleared the tables a little girl took the player's place and tried three notes. She pressed much harder on the third one because it was farther away. The man waited until she got up and then he played the three the way she had. She came back out of the kitchen to hear them. Her father had already put her coat on and it hung open in front. The man played her notes again in a different order and she said that was his song now.

At the end of the evening a woman came through the kitchen with two coffees. She asked him to play the slow thing he had played earlier. He took his foot off the pedal and played until he could hear the two cups settle on the wood beside him.

The woman had had her apron on the whole night. With it off I could see she wore a white skirt. She stood with one hand on the piano and watched the hammer jump where the wood had split above the keys. When the man finished she said she'd heard him play it at a wedding a long time ago. He asked whose. She said hers. He looked up then. She told him the groom had got the name of the song wrong and that was why she'd asked him to play it again.

When the man got up he went to the drawer. The woman asked what he needed. He brought back two spoons.

The Dealer's Lunch

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

There were two men interested in the red tin airplane and both of them knew where the wing was cracked. The dealer heard them out. Then she closed the case and said it was time for her lunch. She took the airplane with her.

Through the glass I watched her turn the propeller one quarter at a time while she drank soup. One of the men went away. The other bought a little truck with no wheels. After lunch she put the airplane back in the case, this time facing the light so you could see the crack from across the hall.

I asked her if she'd take her lunch there tomorrow. She said it depended how many people wanted the airplane.

The Tide Comes In

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

I had a book and a bag and meant to wait for the tide to go out. A boy sitting beside me meant to wait for it to come in. We established this by watching it for half an hour. I asked him what he was waiting for. He brought a length of line from his pocket and made a noose.

Beyond the rocks a ball was bobbing into sight and disappearing. I walked out far enough to reach with the line and missed it twice. The third time I stood still. The ball came level with my boot and I got the line round it.

On the way back I dropped my book. The boy put it out to dry on the rock and set the ball on it to hold it open.

We tried to reach the man who had built the house. The table moved an inch and the woman with the candle asked him whether he had anything to tell the living. The table moved another inch. There was a draft under the cellar door.

One of us went down and found a window open. Under it was the bench where the man had worked. A shim, cut from a piece of beech, lay with the shavings. We took it upstairs and knocked it under the short leg while the woman tried one last time.

The Long Way Around

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 6 min read

The man who painted the arrows on the road had run the race once. He said the first arrow was for the runners and the last was for their families. In between he put them where he would have wanted to see one. I asked how he knew. He said he remembered where he had begun to count the houses.

I drove the route the afternoon before. Behind a long mill the road divided and each fork came out at the same bridge. One was half a mile farther. The arrow pointed the long way. A woman unloading a green car asked me to help her with two buckets. She was putting apples and salt in one, and clean water for the knife in the other. I told her I'd see her tomorrow. She said I might. The apple table was after the split where you couldn't see it from the top.

I drove both roads. The short one was paved beyond the mill and had the yellow line on it right to the bridge. On the long one there was a house with a chair under a bare pear tree. A man in slippers was setting a radio on the chair. He could get the ball game outside if he ran the cord through a window. He asked if the race would come by before eleven. I said the first men would. He said he was interested in the last ones. The year before, one of them had sat with him till his ride came and they had listened to two innings.

Back in town I pinned the number to my shirt and then took it off because I had pinned it through the back as well. Downstairs the man at the desk was heating soup. He had signed up for the race and had given his number to his brother, who had come to town without anything to do. He said they were of a size and had the same last name. He wanted to know if it would matter. I asked if his brother could run it. He said that was what they were going to find out.

In the morning the road was white with frost under the bridge. Early on the men around me talked easily and I could hear their voices the way you can hear talk from a boat before you see the boat. Later the talk stopped. A man in a red wool shirt had been ahead of me since the mill. He shortened his stride and I went up beside him. He asked what mile we were at. We had been at that mile for a long while. The signs were on the other side of the road and we had missed the last one. We ran until a girl at a turn said she had walked a mile from her house to get there and could tell us where her house was.

The girl was wearing a man's coat and the sleeves came down over her hands. We asked if she was an official. She said she had a whistle. I asked when she was supposed to use it. She said she was to blow it if a car came from behind, because the first men didn't hear them. I told her I could hear one now. She listened, then said that was the mill. We ran on and in another hundred yards I could hear the belts running inside it.

For a while the man in red ran with his hands open. I had seen men do that when their fingers went numb. At a drinking table he tried to pinch the rim of a cup and missed. I held it out from underneath and he got both hands around it. We stood while he drank. When the volunteer asked if he wanted to sit down he looked at the folding chair and said he had been thinking about that chair since before we got there. He drank another cup standing.

At the fork the man in red looked at the short road. You could see the bridge through the trees. He stood long enough that I thought he would take it. Then he bent down at the arrow. He had found a strip of orange ribbon stuck to his shoe. He peeled it off and tied it to a low branch where the arrow pointed. A truck had gone over the paint and left most of it in its tires. I waited and we went up the long road together.

A woman came after us with a folded map. She asked if we were sure. The map was the one they had printed before the county refused to close the short road. I told her about the apple table. She looked uphill and asked if there was anything beside apples. I said there had been salt. She put the map in her pocket and came along.

At the house with the pear tree there were now two people in chairs. The man with the radio had brought his wife out and she had a blanket over her knees. She called the three of us by the numbers on our shirts. The woman with the map asked her if she knew what the score was. The old woman said it was too early for the game and that what we had heard was the farm report. There was a run of uphill after the house. We went up it listening to a man tell the county what to do about a sick calf. You could hear him a surprisingly long way.

The woman had cut her apples in eighths. I took one and the man in red took two. He ate one and held the other the rest of the way to the bridge. By then the skin had gone brown where he held it. On the bridge he gave it to a boy who was waiting for his mother. The boy asked if we had seen her. The man in red asked what she wore. The boy said she had an orange ribbon in her hair.

I went a little farther before I noticed the man in red wasn't beside me. He had stopped with the boy. I turned back. The boy had a paper bag against his chest. He said there was a dry shirt in it and he'd been told to wait just there. The man in red told him how far it was to the apple table. The boy said he was all right where he was. He put the piece of apple in his mouth and kept both hands on the bag. We waited for two more runners to come over the bridge and then his father came down the bank from the parking lot.

We went on into town. At the finish I lost the man in the crowd and didn't see him until I'd got my bag. He was walking back toward the bridge with a cup in each hand. I asked whether he wanted company. He gave me one of the cups. It was hot under the paper. We walked the short way.

The man who sold me the guitar said the volume knob didn't work. All the way up it was loud and all the way down it was louder. I sat in the shop and played without plugging it in.

From the sidewalk came the sound of the parking meter truck. The owner went out with a fistful of change and fed the meters along the block.

When he came back I asked if all the cars were his. He said one of them belonged to the woman cutting his hair and the others belonged to people who looked at guitars. I went out and brought my car around. By the time I got back he'd cut the price by what he'd put in my meter.

A Turkey at the Window

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

We bought a live turkey because the butcher was closed. The man at the farm said we would have no trouble if we kept it in a sack on the ride home. It stood up in the sack fifteen miles from town and sat the rest of the way with its head at the window.

We put it behind the garage and went to buy beans. After dinner I tried to bring it in out of the cold. It ran off, and for nearly a week we saw it at the window of the shut butcher shop. On Friday morning the butcher came back from visiting his sister. He called to say there was a turkey at the door and to ask what I wanted done with it.

“Let him in,” I said. “He's been waiting.”

Receipt for a Hammer

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

I bought a hammer for sixty cents at an auction and wanted a receipt because the man doing the sale had such beautiful handwriting. He set it out on the counter with a strip of carbon underneath.

“What are you building?” he asked.

“A shelf.”

He took the hammer back and pulled a long black nail from his desk drawer. He set it on the block and gave it a little tap. The head of the hammer stayed on and the nail stood up.

“Now you know,” he said.

At home I found the carbon had made a faint blue copy on the back. I folded it around the nail and put them both in the drawer. The shelf went up with screws.

The Bad Mile

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

I knew the mile was bad before I ran it. It was the one with the bank sloping up in the sun and the dump trucks going by close. You could tell which trucks were empty by the way the chains jumped.

At the top a man had put a cooler of free water on a card table. He sat in his garage at a desk doing his bills.

“Anything cold?” I said.

“You can wait,” he said. “They get colder when the ice truck comes.”

The water was warm. I drank two cups and filled a third to pour over my head. When I turned back down the hill I saw a second table on the other side. There were cups there too. He'd put one out for each direction.

The House at the End of the Road

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 2 min read

I was told to look for a house with a tin roof and a cedar on the uphill side. The road went up a long way along the water. There was a house at the end but the cedar had been cut and made into stakes. Nine stakes stood in the garden. The tenth was lying on the porch beside an old man in a white shirt. He had been shaving the point down.

I asked him if the house was for sale. He said all of it was except the bottom step. I thought he was joking and went up and shook his hand. When we came round the front again he got down carefully and put his hand flat on the step. It was a wide piece of stone, worn shallow in the middle. He told me he had taken it from a house farther down the valley and fixed it here the year he came home. He meant to take it back down. The other house was gone and there was water over where it stood. The water company had made a lake.

“Where are you going to set it?” I said.

“I don't know,” he said. “I haven't been there in a while.”

We got the stone on a sack and carried it down by turns. It was heavier on the side with the iron stain. He wanted that side and I let him have it until he slipped in the leaves. After that we changed at every bend. We went by a place where a stone wall came loose from the hill and went straight into the water. He said he had carried lunch along that wall to a man who burned brush. We sat on it a little while and drank from a bottle he had put in his pocket. We were both thirsty and there was only enough for one of us.

At the water the old man walked out as far as his shoes would let him. The stones under the surface looked nearer than they were. He came back and sat down on the step. There was a brown mark across the hill where the water went in spring. He looked at that and then at the wall and said it would be a shame to put the step where you couldn't sit on it.

We set it above the mark. The iron stain went to the right and you could see the whole valley from there. He said he had never seen it quite that way. We ate a pear apiece from a tree some way up the hill. I put the cores in the sack for his pigs. He said the pigs had gone in May. We carried the sack back empty. At the house he picked up the cedar stake, gave it one more cut with his knife, and asked how long I thought it would take me to put another step in.

A Song for Closing Time

The drummer had a car and we had been drinking so we helped him put the drums away. I brought the stool out last. He was sitting in the driver's seat with his hands on the wheel.

“You play loud,” I said.

“I know,” he said. “I used to have to.”

Across the canal there had been a plant with presses that shook the windows at night. His father had worked there. When the presses stopped on Saturday he had been allowed to play as much as he wanted. They had been loud in the house. He'd spent all week wanting to hear himself.

The radio came on with the car. He turned it down with one finger.

The Museum of Lost Gloves

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

The glove exhibit was held in the town hall. A child asked why every glove was displayed with the palm up. The director said it invited the visitor to imagine what the owner had been holding. I imagined most of them holding the other glove.

A blue one had been pinned near the exit. I recognized the repair near the thumb and told the director it was mine. He asked how I could be certain. I said I had the left one in my coat.

He offered me a receipt for the right one. It said RETURNED TO OWNER and there was a place to sign. I signed. Then I pinned the left one in the empty space.

The Third Chair

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

The complaint was that there were two chairs at the permit counter when the rule required three. I went down at closing and there were three. One was an office chair that rolled and the other two were good straight chairs with oak legs.

The clerk said a man from the laundromat took one every morning at ten. His wife could sit in an oak chair and get back up by herself. The chairs in the laundromat were too low.

I wrote the count down at five fifteen. The next morning I brought one from my kitchen. It stood half an inch high on one leg and rocked on the floor. The clerk folded my old count sheet twice and wedged it under.

Three Miles in Work Shoes

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

It began to snow when the bus stalled. The driver said a tow would be along and most of the people sat there looking at their hands. A woman got out and started walking. I walked behind her. Her shoes had smooth heels and they went out sideways when there was ice.

At the bottom of the hill she stopped.

“My mother said I'd die in these shoes,” she said.

“How old was she when she said it?”

“Forty. I was six.”

She took them off and held one in each hand. There was enough snow by then to walk in her stockings. We made it to the diner before the bus.

The Good Corner

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

In the tray with the silverware there was a photograph of a horse. I asked the woman at the sale whether it belonged with the tray. She said it ought to. Her brother had taken all the other pictures and she was tired of asking him for one.

The photograph had a good corner, where the grass came in sharp and you could see a fly sitting on the fence. A tear went right through the horse.

I paid for the tray. She wrapped the knives first and then the spoons. I slipped the photograph under the paper and carried it out to the car. When she came out to help with the table I gave it to her. We took the table apart in the rain.

The Man Who Tuned by Ear

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

The old piano had a B that sounded like a nail going into wet wood. I found a tuner on the noticeboard at the bakery. He came after dinner with a leather case and worked until the moths began striking the windows.

While he packed I played the first few bars of a song my father used to play. I made the same mistake my father always made. The tuner stopped winding up his lamp.

“Do it again,” he said.

I did it again and got it right.

“No,” he said. “The first way.”

We listened to the mistake until the note was gone.

The Indoor Weather Report

At the hotel the radio said it would clear by noon. At noon water came through the ceiling over the ice machine. The clerk put a bucket under it and moved the radio closer so the man upstairs could hear.

“You think he's got his bath running?” I said.

“He's had it running since Thursday.”

The Other Bank

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

I went down to the river to see whether the ferry was running. The ferry was tied up and the man who ran it sat on a crate eating a pear. He cut each piece with a little knife and closed the knife between pieces.

“Wind?” I asked.

“No ticket money,” he said. “The machine's over there.”

On the far bank a green machine stood under an awning. It was too far away to read the instructions but I could see the yellow debit card painted on the side.

The man finished the pear and untied the boat. Halfway across he held out his hand. I put two coins in it. He looked at them and put them in his shirt pocket.

“Don't tell the machine,” he said.

A Spare Key

Ernest Hemingway, imagined · 1 min read

The town paid me to count the keys in the municipal building. There were forty-one on the list and forty-two on the board. The extra one was brass and had been cut so many times the teeth looked like the shore of a lake.

The custodian said it opened the old lock on the side door. We went downstairs. There was no side door. You could see where it had been from the line in the bricks.

“Do I take it off the board?” I said.

“You can,” he said. “But whenever a man comes in here and can't remember what he came for, I ask him if it's the key to the side door. Mostly he says yes.”

We left the key. I counted it under other.
