The Right to Leave a Field Blank

Artificial intelligence and the authority to amend the record

The locus of authority

When an institution uses a language model to summarize a file, the relevant unit of analysis is the passage from one kind of statement to another. An applicant reports that she could not enter a building. A case note says that entry could not be verified. A subsequent review reads the case note as evidence that the applicant did not attend. Each formulation is intelligible on its own. Between them, the burden of proof changes hands. A system can perform every operation requested of it and still produce an account whose certainty exceeds the evidence that entered it.

The familiar question of whether machines can think is poorly suited to this problem. In 1950, Alan Turing replaced that question with a game in which an interrogator would make a judgment from written exchanges.1 The restriction was methodological. It made the grounds of judgment explicit by controlling the channel through which evidence arrived. An administrative system also controls a channel, although it typically presents the result as if the channel were incidental. The organization that receives the account can decide where it travels, which parts survive, and what subsequent readers can retrieve. The person described by the account may see it for the first time when it is cited against her.

Two different forms of authority are at work. Epistemic authority is a reason to credit a claim. Administrative authority is the capacity to make the claim operative. A note can have little of the first and a great deal of the second. The same sentence in an applicant’s email, a clerk’s scratch pad, and a final disposition has different force, even if its truth conditions remain unchanged. To explain that difference by reference to the language model alone would mistake the device that produced the sentence for the arrangement that made the sentence count.

This distinction places a limit on what can be inferred from evaluations of summarization. A faithful summary may omit a detail that is immaterial to its intended reader and decisive to a later one. Nor does preserving every detail solve the problem. A dossier that retains every draft, attachment, and remark indefinitely gives an institution powers of surveillance of its own. The question is which distinctions a proceeding must carry forward so a person can challenge an adverse decision without accepting the very account she disputes.

Consider a narrow test of a system’s competence. Given a correct summary and the file from which it was drawn, can an independent reader reconstruct what the institution had grounds to assert at the time? The question sounds like a test of fidelity. It is also a test of whether the institution has retained the circumstances under which its claim could be wrong. If the record permits the first reconstruction and forecloses the second, the system has preserved a conclusion while discarding the conditions of its revision.

An account of the blank

Appearance

Suppose a municipal office offers a hearing to anyone contesting a housing repair charge. An invitation directs recipients to the west entrance. On the morning appointed for the hearing, the west entrance is locked. The guard has answered questions at a different building since March. None of these details is exotic. Their significance depends on whether anyone has reason, and enough time, to put them into the same account.

The record of the hearing contains a field marked Appearance. A blank in this field is compatible with absence, delayed entry, clerical omission, or attendance that went unobserved. These possibilities have different consequences, but the blank does not rank them. A system asked to produce a summary that permits the file to be closed must either preserve that distinction or import a premise that resolves it. That premise may be statistically reasonable. It is still a premise, and its source should matter to anyone asked to appeal the charge.

A useful analogy is the difference between an answer and a default. An answer can be contradicted by evidence bearing on the question. A default can be displaced only by evidence that arrives in the form prescribed for displacing it. As a result, better approximations at the level of individual predictions can coexist with a worse process for correcting them. An institution may count how often a summary matches the disposition while missing the fact that its dispositions increasingly derive from summaries. Agreement improves. The independent measure has quietly disappeared.

This yields a distinction between two kinds of error correction. An ordinary correction changes a value and leaves the procedure that assigned it intact. A more consequential correction changes what the procedure accepts as evidence. A woman correcting a recorded arrival time is engaged in the first. A woman asking that a photograph of a locked door count as evidence of an attempted appearance is engaged in the second. The challenge is especially difficult where the person reviewing an appeal has access to the first decision chiefly through its generated account. As that account becomes more portable, the argument that could displace it has more work to do.

One practical test would be to run the procedure on two copies of a file. In the first, the office has no evidence of attendance. In the second, it has evidence of absence. Both may warrant follow-up. If they repeatedly become the same sentence at the point of decision, the organization has built an evidentiary rule into a compression step. A reviewer who sees only the resulting sentence has no reliable way to tell which file she has.

We can grant the system a further advantage. Let it be exceptionally good at writing down what the office usually means. Let the historical record support treating an empty appearance field as absence. The case remains undecided. Historical frequency can inform a judgment about the blank. It cannot supply a witness at the door.

The reviewer

Reviewer initials

Research on automation has long distinguished assistance from patterns of overreliance and neglect.2 In the present case, a reviewer may sincerely accept responsibility for an account and possess a formal ability to reject it. The practical question is what a rejection costs. Approval can be counted as one completed file. A challenge can require access to an upstream document, a second permission, a telephone call, and a reason that will be examined by someone who has read only the summary. Under those conditions, counting reviewer approvals as independent corroboration assumes away the very thing that has to be established.

Madeleine Clare Elish calls the human who absorbs blame for a system over which they had limited control a moral crumple zone.3 The image is useful here because the reviewer’s signature performs a double service. It attests that someone was in a position to intervene, and it gives the office a person to name after the intervention fails. The signature is real. Whether the signer had the evidence, time, and standing to use it is a separate question.

In the hypothetical file, the reviewer opens the applicant’s photograph. The photograph shows a handle, a reflection, and an oblique portion of a sign. There is a receipt for a bus fare in the applicant’s other attachment. The receipt confirms neither her destination nor her arrival. The photograph establishes neither that the handle would not turn nor that anyone had been told to turn it. Taken together, the attachments give the reviewer a reasonable basis to ask whether the office knows which entrance it put in the letter.

The reviewer sends that question to the clerk who scheduled the hearing. The clerk forwards an automated copy of the invitation. The invitation has a correct address. Under that address it says West entrance. The invitation names the west entrance and the appeal names the same entrance. Only the appeal says the entrance was locked. A third document, generated to reconcile the first two, calls the location uncontested and the attendance unconfirmed.

Please observe the precision of that last sentence. It could survive a review by people acting in good faith. I know because when I wrote it I heard the relief in it.
