The Drawer You Leave Until Last

At the bottom of a kitchen drawer there is usually a key nobody can place. It looks enough like a house key that throwing it out feels premature. You try it on the back door and the shed. Then you put it back, having done just enough detective work to justify keeping it another ten years.

Clear out the whole drawer and the key ends up beside a rubber band that breaks as soon as you stretch it, a takeout menu from a place that closed, and a recipe card with one brown corner. The menu has a phone number you still know by heart. The recipe says to bake until done. Somebody knew you would ask at about twenty minutes and that they would be in the next room.

Years later you make the recipe and spend the last five minutes crouched in front of the oven. The edges look right, but the middle still moves. You give it two minutes more. The card goes back in the drawer with a little flour on it. Next time the part about when it’s done will make more sense.

A baseball card checklist is unusually helpful about what goes where. You get a number, a name, and a box you can tick. Then you find two copies of the same card and have to choose. One has cleaner corners. On the other, a kid filled in a team name on the back with a blue ballpoint. It takes longer to decide than you thought it would. The checklist is happy either way.

In “A Spare Key,” a man is paid to count the keys in a municipal building. He finds one more than the town has on paper. The custodian knows what it opened and shows him the line in the bricks where the door used to be. That would be enough for the count. Then the custodian tells him what he does with the key now, and the man has to decide what to write down.

By the time you finish the kitchen drawer, three pens work and there’s enough room for them to roll when you shut it. The menu goes in the recycling. The recipe card goes near the front, where you can get at it without sorting through everything else. The key is still on the counter. Before you put it away, you try it once in the front door. It turns. Of course it does. For ten years you’d thought of it as the one key in the house you didn’t need.
